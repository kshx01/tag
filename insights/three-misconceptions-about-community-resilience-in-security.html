Three Misconceptions About Community Resilience in Security

As security threats become increasingly complex — from cyberattacks and terrorism to misinformation, civil unrest, and climate-related emergencies — the concept of community resilience has become central to modern security thinking. Governments, law enforcement agencies and security professionals now recognise that resilient communities are often the first line of defence against disruption and instability.

Yet, despite its importance, community resilience in security is frequently misunderstood. These misconceptions can weaken preparedness, damage trust and create gaps in response.

Here are three common misconceptions about community resilience in a security context and why they matter.

Misconception 1: Security Is the Responsibility of Authorities Alone

A common myth is that maintaining security is solely the responsibility of governments, police, intelligence agencies or emergency services. Under this view, communities are passive recipients of protection rather than active contributors to resilience.

The Reality

Modern security relies heavily on community participation, trust and cooperation. Communities often identify risks, share local knowledge, report suspicious activity, support vulnerable individuals and help counter misinformation long before authorities can intervene effectively.

This is especially important in areas such as counterterrorism, cyber awareness, public health emergencies and disaster response. Security agencies increasingly depend on strong relationships with communities to detect emerging threats and maintain social stability.

Why This Matters

If communities are excluded from security planning, trust can erode and critical warning signs may be missed. At the same time, expecting communities to manage threats without institutional support is equally problematic.

Effective security resilience depends on partnership, where authorities provide leadership, resources and coordination while communities contribute local insight, social cohesion and collective action.

Misconception 2: Resilience Means Returning to “Normal” After a Security Incident

After a cyberattack, terrorist event or major disruption, resilience is often described as the ability to “bounce back” quickly and restore normal operations.

The Reality

Resilience is not only about recovery, but also about adaptation and learning. Returning to pre-incident conditions may leave the same vulnerabilities exposed.

For example, following a terrorist incident, resilience is not only achieved simply by reopening public spaces or restoring transport services. Communities and authorities need to strengthen information-sharing, improve emergency coordination, enhance public awareness campaigns, and address social tensions or extremist narratives that contributed to the threat environment in the first place.

Similarly, incidents involving radicalisation or targeted violence often reveal the importance of long-term community engagement, trust-building and early intervention strategies, not just immediate recovery.

Why This Matters

A narrow focus on recovery can create a cycle of repeated vulnerability. Resilience should involve continuous improvement, preparedness and adaptation to evolving threats.

Communities that learn from disruption are far better equipped to respond to future challenges.

Misconception 3: All Communities Are Equally Resilient to Security Threats

Security strategies are often designed with the assumption that all communities have similar capacities to respond to crises.

The Reality

Resilience varies significantly depending on social, economic and structural inequalities. Some communities have greater access to emergency information, education, healthcare and trusted institutions than others.

For example, communities that experience social exclusion or low trust in authorities may be more vulnerable during a security incident or period of civil unrest because messaging or public safety guidance may not be received, trusted or acted upon effectively. Similarly, some areas may have fewer resources to recover from prolonged security disruptions.

Social fragmentation, economic instability and exclusion can all weaken a community’s ability to respond effectively to security threats.

Why This Matters

Security resilience strategies that ignore inequality risk leaving the most vulnerable groups exposed. A one-size-fits-all approach can unintentionally deepen existing divisions and undermine trust.

Inclusive security planning, including accessible communication, community engagement, education, and targeted support, is essential for building resilience across society.

—

Community resilience is not just about physical ability to protect and respond, it is about building communities that can anticipate, adapt to and recover from a wide range of threats.

Community resilience should not be viewed as a replacement for formal security structures, nor as a simplistic ability to “bounce back.” Instead, it is a shared process built on trust, inclusion, preparedness and collaboration.

The strongest security strategies are those that recognise communities not as passive victims of risk, but as active partners in creating safer, more adaptable societies. TAG works across diverse community and government structures to strengthen trust, coordination and collaboration between local populations and public institutions. By bringing together stakeholders from across the security ecosystem, including local authorities, emergency responders, community leaders and civil society organisations, we support communities to become more connected and resilient, ensuring they are better prepared to respond to evolving threats.
